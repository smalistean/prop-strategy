The Weekend Fade

A stock market that sleeps, a copy of it that doesn't, and a rule for the gap between them. Every result so far is on this page — the losses too.

Binance sells contracts that follow real US stocks — Tesla, Apple, Coinbase, about two dozen of them. The real stock market closes on Friday at 4pm New York time and opens again Monday at 9:30. The Binance contracts trade all weekend anyway.

With the real market shut there is no true price to lean on, and nobody can hedge. So the Binance price drifts on thin weekend trading. Some names drift down. On Monday morning the real market opens, and the contract moves back toward where the stock actually is.

I buy the ones that drifted down, on Sunday evening. I sell them on Monday at 11am New York.

The rule, in five lines

  • Friday 4pm NYWrite down every stock's closing price. That is the reference for the weekend.
  • Sunday 8pm UTCAny name that is more than 0.5% below Friday's close gets bought. Three-quarters of the account goes in, split equally across them, and never more than a fifth of the account on any one name — so one name means 20% in, two means 40%, three means 60%, four or more means the full 75%. Nothing is chosen by feel.
  • Two skips, onlyA company is skipped if it reports earnings before Monday's exit, or if it put out real news over the weekend. Nothing else skips a name.
  • Monday 11am NYSell everything. No stop-losses, no early exits, no holding on.
  • AlwaysThe rule was written down and frozen before the first trade. A bad weekend doesn't change it. Neither does a good one.

Backtest bars are what the rule would have made on a $5,000 account, sized exactly as the rule sizes: one name gets 20% of the account, two get 40%, three get 60%, four or more get the full 75%. That is why a big percentage on a single name is still a small bar — the rule never puts much on one name. Live bars are what my account actually made. On 09-04 I put in $300 where the rule said about $1,000, so that bar is smaller than the rule's would have been.

The backtest adds up to +$661.26 over twenty weekends — +$33.06 per weekend, about +0.66% of the account each time. In practice thin markets would have trimmed roughly an eighth of that at this size, so read the total as nearer +$575 than +$661. It comes from twenty weekends and could be luck — the standard statistical test puts that at roughly one chance in twelve. Three live weekends can't confirm it or deny it. They are the beginning of the answer, not the answer.

Why does this exist? Why hasn't someone bigger taken it?

Because it is small. The whole thing can absorb perhaps $300–500 thousand before extra money stops earning anything. A fund doesn't get out of bed for that.

It is also awkward. The decision has to be made at one fixed minute on a Sunday evening, across many names at once, and unwound at another fixed minute on Monday — every week, without exception. And it lives in an odd corner — stock contracts on a crypto exchange — that most stock investors never look at.

What is left for one person is exactly what is too small and too annoying for a company.

What can go wrong

  • One name can gap. AXTI lost 12.8% in a single weekend this July. The rule holds it anyway, so that loss is real.
  • Some weekends everything falls together. Eight of the twenty-three weekends lost money. The worst cost $107.55 on a $5,000 account — about 2.2% of it.
  • It can stop working, quietly. If enough people start doing the same thing, the weekend drift shrinks. I record the inputs every week to catch that — but at this sample size I would only know months after it happened.
  • It depends on me. The rule is fixed, but someone has to run it every single week, and that someone is me.
  • It depends on Binance. They can change the contracts or shut them down, and they have done both to other names.
